Opinion column

Batteries absorbed the growth, not the surplus

Between January and August 2026 Chile's grid batteries charged 2,017 GWh more than a year earlier. Over the same period, solar and wind curtailment fell 4%.

Solar and wind generation grew by 2,887 GWh over that window, and seven in ten of those GWh went into charging batteries. Storage absorbed the growth, not the surplus, and the remaining curtailment is shifting towards areas without batteries.

Carlos Suazo Martínez Chief Executive, SPEC September 2026 Updated 20 October 12 min read

Curtailment is no longer a marginal phenomenon in the operation of Chile's national grid. In 2020 the grid failed to inject 247 GWh of solar and wind energy, 1.8% of what those plants could have produced. In 2025 it was 6,044 GWh and 15.6%: one in every six available renewable megawatt-hours never reached the network.

Against that backdrop, 2026 shows signs of improvement. The battery fleet in commercial operation and testing went from 3,300 to 7,100 MW between December and September, the January-to-August curtailment rate fell from 13.4% to 11.5%, and June recorded 2.5%, the lowest month since mid-2022. As of 18 October the Coordinador counts 4,007 GWh curtailed in the year, 3.9% less than at the same date in 2025.

Curtailment did fall, but considerably less than the scale of the new storage would suggest. The hourly data, the month-by-month evolution and the geographic distribution help explain why.

First: six years and a factor of twenty-four

The monthly series is clear. Between 2020 and 2025 curtailment grew twenty-four fold and the rate went from 1.8% to 15.6%. The worst month in the series is November 2024, at 26.6%: more than a quarter of that month's available solar and wind energy was not injected.

In 2025 it was the rate, not the volume, that stabilised: 6,044 GWh against 5,643 in 2024, at a rate of 15.6% against 15.9%. The system was curtailing more energy in absolute terms at a similar share, because the renewable fleet was growing as fast as curtailment.

The 2025 and 2026 curves separate during winter and run parallel again in spring, even though the battery fleet doubled from one year to the next.

Second: curtailment is concentrated in daylight hours

Averaged over the year, curtailment looks like a diffuse phenomenon. Hour by hour it is not: 97% of it occurs between 8 am and 7 pm, with the peak always in the same afternoon band.

In 2021 the peak was 59 MW at three in the afternoon. In 2026 it is 1,460 MW at the same hour, twenty-five times more. This year's change of shape is also visible: midday curtailment decreases while the morning and evening shoulders increase —at five in the afternoon, from 876 to 1,045 MW—. The curve widened instead of falling.

97%of curtailment occurs between 8 am and 7 pm. At night the system barely curtails: the surplus is concentrated in daylight hours.

Third: the growth of storage

In January 2023 the grid had 84 MW of storage in operation, spread across four installations, discharging 0.4 GWh a month. In September 2026 there are 64 plants and 7,111 MW in commercial operation and testing, discharging 579 GWh in the month. Few technologies have scaled at that pace in three and a half years.

The operating pattern is as expected: batteries charge at midday and discharge into the evening peak. Between 10 am and 3 pm the fleet absorbs energy at an average of more than 1,400 MW, and between 7 and 11 pm it returns it to the system.

Fourth: where the new energy went

Between January and August 2026 the grid's batteries charged 3,231 GWh, 2,017 more than in the same period of 2025. Over the same window, solar and wind curtailment was 2,994 GWh against 3,125 the year before: a 4% decrease.

The reason the decrease is limited lies in supply growth. January-to-August solar and wind generation increased by 2,887 GWh over 2025. Additional battery charging took 2,017 of those GWh —seven in ten— and the rest of the system absorbed the balance. Curtailment fell by 132 GWh.

70%of 2026's additional solar and wind generation went into charging batteries. Storage mainly absorbed the year's growth, not the surplus that already existed.

The breakdown by technology refines this reading. Solar curtailment fell 12% and, in the middle hours of the day —eleven to three—, 24%: that is where the effect of batteries can be seen. Wind curtailment, by contrast, rose 20%, spread across the whole day.

The contrast shows both the reach and the limit of storage. Where the surplus is concentrated in a few hours, as at the solar midday, the battery shifts it to the night. But curtailment is structurally a system-wide problem: overproduction relative to what the system consumes and can manage. In those hours, units that cannot be turned down remain dispatched —thermal plants at technical minimum and hydro plants bound by water use and, in the summer season, by irrigation agreements— and the variable generation that does not fit on top of that base is curtailed. Wind curtailment, spread across the day, also occurs in hours when batteries are already full or discharging.

Fifth: spring as the test

The year-to-date total hides marked seasonality. In January and February 2026, at the height of summer, the system curtailed more than the year before: 21% and 45% more. In winter the relationship reversed: June curtailed 77% less, July 25% less and August 35% less. With spring, when curtailment rises again, the gap narrows: September came in 11% below 2025, and October, eighteen days in, is running 10% above.

The explanation is one of scale. In winter, the daily solar surplus is small enough for a six- or seven-gigawatt fleet to store almost all of it. In spring the surplus is several times larger and batteries fill up before the afternoon ends: in October they charge about 19 GWh a day, and the system still curtails 26 GWh a day.

Wet hydrology adds to this. In August and September hydroelectric generation was 57% higher than in 2025 and thermal output halved. Hydro itself recorded curtailment: 67.6 GWh in August, the highest since December 2024. In its months of highest supply, the system has little additional thermal generation left to displace.

What lies ahead may weigh more than what has been observed. Given the snowpack accumulated in the basins, a very marked melt season is expected, with levels of hydroelectric output not seen in recent years. Whether curtailment actually falls in 2026 remains to be seen: the decrease accumulated to October could reverse in the months of highest sun and highest river flow.

Sixth: where curtailment occurs, and where it is shifting

Eight of every ten GWh curtailed in 2025 were concentrated in two regions: Antofagasta and Atacama accounted for 81%, up from 72% in 2022.

In 2026 that distribution began to change. Between January and August the north —from Arica to Atacama— curtailed 10% less than in 2025, while from Coquimbo southwards curtailment increased 37%, with rises in every region. Batteries, by contrast, are concentrated in the north: 92% of the energy they charged this year corresponds to installations between Arica and Atacama.

At plant level, the effect is less clear. The nine plants whose battery came online in 2026 barely changed their curtailment rate —from 16.9% to 16.1%—, in line with the seventy-five without a battery, which went from 16.3% to 14.8%. Bolero is the exception: with its new battery it dropped from 30% to 9%. The eleven plants whose battery was already operating in 2025 did reduce their rate, from 19.1% to 10.9%, but their batteries charged practically the same in both years, so that decrease is not explained by storage. Tamaya Solar, for example, stopped curtailing from September 2025 with a battery operating just as before.

The effect of new batteries shows up at system level —in the 24% drop in midday solar curtailment and in the decline in the north, where charging is concentrated— rather than in the rate of the plant each battery is attached to. That is consistent with how they operate: a battery charging in the hours of greatest surplus relieves its whole electrical neighbourhood, not just its own plant. For the same reason, the remaining curtailment tends to grow where there are no batteries.

Seventh: zero marginal cost

Curtailment is the energy the system does not take. A zero marginal cost reflects the value of energy the system does take but does not need. They are two sides of the same condition, and the second can be measured precisely: the share of hours in the month with a marginal cost of zero.

In 2019 this phenomenon was practically non-existent. In 2025 Crucero recorded a zero marginal cost in 36% of the year's hours, with months at 46%. What is new in 2026 is geographic. During the first half the four busbars behaved much as in 2025; in winter the south decoupled: Charrúa, in Biobío, recorded a zero marginal cost in 58% of August's hours and 62% of September's, the highest in the series for any of the four busbars, while Crucero and the central zone stood at around 40%.

It is the same geography as the previous section, expressed in prices. The decoupling indicates that in those hours transmission towards the centre ran at its limit, but the origin is the same system-wide surplus: in a wet winter, hydro and wind generation in the south added to a base that could not be turned down, in an area with very little storage.

The value of the energy injected

There is a consequence that does not appear in curtailment statistics, because it is not lost energy but energy delivered at a very low price. The spot revenue per MWh captured by the grid's solar fleet fell to 1.6 USD/MWh in November 2024, and in September 2026 it returned to that level: 1.9 USD/MWh. In 2025 it averaged 12.0 USD/MWh, against a marginal cost of between 50 and 60 USD/MWh at the reference busbars.

A solar plant with no contract and no battery sells, in the months of highest curtailment, at less than a twentieth of the system's average price. Curtailment reduces its volume; zero marginal cost reduces the value of the volume it does deliver. This second loss is considerably larger than the first and receives less attention.

The battery shifts energy; system rigidity sets the surplus

The two central findings of this column —storage absorbing growth and curtailment that persists— stem from the same distinction.

Every megawatt of battery raises the bar of what the system can absorb at midday. But the surplus does not depend on that bar alone: it also depends on how much generation remains dispatched without being able to turn down. As long as the inflexible base —thermal at technical minimum, hydro committed by water use and irrigation— and variable generation grow together, storage reduces the surplus in the hours it can cover, but does not eliminate it.

What to watch from here

There are 4,932 MW of batteries under construction according to the CNE registry, with 21,177 MWh of storage —about 4.3 hours of average duration— and 4,477 of those megawatts estimated to connect in 2026 and 2027. That is two thirds of today's fleet, and three of every four of those megawatts are located between Arica and Atacama.

  • Storage keeps pace with growth, but does not solve it on its own. Seven in ten new GWh of solar and wind generation ended up in batteries. For curtailment to fall in a sustained way, storage has to grow faster than variable generation, and in spring that is not yet happening.
  • Curtailment is a system-wide problem. It reflects overproduction relative to what the system consumes and can manage, with thermal units at technical minimum and hydro plants bound by water use and irrigation. Wind curtailment rose 20% and curtailment from Coquimbo southwards 37%, in a year when the battery fleet doubled: storage is part of the solution, not all of it.
  • Location matters more than total capacity. The effect of a battery shows up in the area where it operates, not necessarily in the plant it is attached to. And the pipeline under construction repeats today's geography: three of every four megawatts go to the north, where curtailment is already falling, and not to the south, where it is rising.
  • Snowmelt will be this year's test. The accumulated snowpack points to a season of hydroelectric output not seen in recent years, in the same months of highest solar radiation. Whether 2026's decrease in curtailment holds through December remains to be seen.
  • The relevant indicator is the monthly rate. Volume rises with the fleet, and the year-to-date total combines a favourable winter with a spring that is not. The monthly rate over available energy is the series that shows whether the system is improving or merely growing.
